### The two rows that matter most

**71.4% answerable against 43.8% resolved is a 27.6 point delivery gap.**
CloudServe do not have an answer problem. They have a problem getting answers
that already exist in front of customers. This is the sentence the rest of the
project is built on, and it is the difference between building a chatbot that
invents answers and building a retrieval system that delivers existing ones.

**The non-fluent segment contradicted the person who raised it.** Sofia
believed those customers had the worst outcomes and that nobody had noticed.
The record says the opposite. The value of checking is that it changes what we
build: had we taken her account on trust we would have built a special path
that the evidence does not justify, and we would have missed the tier gap,
which is real.

## Section three: where the time actually goes

Effort is approximated by total recorded resolution minutes per intent, the
only effort signal the corpus carries. Volume and effort diverge sharply.

| Ticket category | Share of volume | Share of effort | Why the two differ | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| security_incident | 5.2% | **9.69%** | 786 min mean. Every one needs a human and half have no documented answer. | `effort_by_intent` |
| compliance_request | 5.2% | **8.38%** | 679 min mean. Contractual and legal review. | `effort_by_intent` |
| data_residency | 5.8% | **7.08%** | 515 min mean. Daniel: "we get those wrong occasionally even as humans." | `effort_by_intent` |
| feature_request | 4.0% | **6.87%** | 724 min mean and **0% answerable** — there is no article, so time is spent discovering there is no answer. | `effort_by_intent` |
| deployment_failure | 5.4% | 6.18% | 483 min mean, but 70.4% answerable. | `effort_by_intent` |
| rate_limit | 2.6% | **0.75%** | 122 min mean, 92.3% answerable. Cheap and documented. | `effort_by_intent` |
| onboarding | 4.4% | 2.11% | 202 min mean, 90.9% answerable. | `effort_by_intent` |
| sso_configuration | 5.2% | 2.36% | 191 min mean, 84.6% answerable. | `effort_by_intent` |

**What this changes.** The four most expensive intents are 20.2% of volume but
**32.0% of effort** — and three of the four are ones the corpus says must never
be automated. Automation cannot touch the expensive work. What it can do is
clear the documented, cheap, high-volume tail so that the expensive work gets
the attention it needs. That reframes the business case from "replace agent
time" to "return agent time to the work that requires a person".

### The steps an agent takes on a typical ticket

Reconstructed from Sofia's account in interview two.

| Step | What the agent does | Roughly how long | Could this be automated? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Open the queue, sort by age, pick the ticket closest to breaching | seconds | Yes, and it should be sorted by urgency rather than age — Ravi's point that a failing deployment and a pagination question sit in the same queue. |
| 2 | Read the ticket and work out what is actually being asked | 1–3 min, longer where phrasing is unclear | Partly. This is what intent classification does, and it is where the system is strongest. |
| 3 | Decide whether she knows the answer confidently enough to send it | under a minute | This is precisely the routing decision, and Sofia's own three criteria — do not know, not confident enough, security — map onto the three escalation rules. |
| 4 | **Find the answer: search the docs, or more often her personal snippet file** | **the bulk of the time** | **Yes, and this is the target.** Sofia does not need the answer supplied; she needs it found. Automating this is the whole project. |
| 5 | Write it out in her own words | 2–5 min | Yes, where the source is a reviewed article. |
| 6 | Send, or escalate with no context attached | seconds | The escalation should carry the analysis. Daniel loses time re-reading threads and re-asking customers questions they have already answered. |

Steps 4 and 5 are where the minutes accumulate and both are automatable.
Step 3 is the one that must remain conservative.

## Section five: data, constraints and risk

| Data source | What it contains | Quality and gaps | Access constraints | Private data present? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Support tickets | 500 dev / 80 validation, with intent, urgency, expected route, answerability, expected sources, and the historic outcome | Fully labelled and internally consistent. Synthetic, and the intents are unusually separable — 99.8% out-of-fold accuracy will not survive real traffic | Development set free to use; validation for checking only; the hidden set is used once | Customer names, ids, region and tier. Names appear in ticket bodies |
| Documentation | 29 reviewed articles | Complete: every article is an expected source somewhere and no expected id is missing. Ines reviews on a rotation, `last_reviewed_days_ago` is present | Free to use | None |
| Past resolutions | 200 senior-agent reference answers with required points and forbidden claims | High quality by construction | Free to use, but **not** a test set | Possible customer references in answer text |
| Agents' private snippet files | Not supplied | **Does not exist as data.** Daniel warns it contains answers correct two years ago and wrong since; Ines has twice asked for consolidation | Not available | Unknown |
| Customer records | Not supplied; tier, region and fluency only, on the ticket | Sufficient for the fairness audit | On the ticket | Yes — tier and region are commercially sensitive |

**The constraint that shaped the design most.** Daniel's warning about the
snippet files is the reason retrieval is restricted to Ines's 29 reviewed
articles and never learns from historic agent responses. Training on what
agents did would scale up the stale answers he describes, and it would do so
invisibly. The cost is that anything not yet documented cannot be answered —
accepted, because that case escalates rather than being guessed at.

### Initial risk register

| What could go wrong | How likely | How bad | Who it affects | First thought on preventing it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| The system answers confidently and incorrectly | Medium | **Severe** — Marcus's stated failure condition; his customers are engineers who "will screenshot a confidently incorrect answer and put it on the internet within the hour" | Customer, then the support team handling the complaint | Calibrated threshold; answers grounded only in retrieved passages; citations checked against what was actually retrieved; block rather than send on any doubt |
| Private information appears in a reply | Low | **Severe** — no acceptable rate | Both customers involved; CloudServe legally | Pattern checks on every outbound response, blocking rather than redacting |
| Some customers get consistently worse answers | **High** | Severe — Ravi warns it "will come up at renewal" | Whichever segment loses | Segment the evaluation from the start; measure by tier, fluency and channel on every run |
| Documentation goes out of date | Medium | Moderate | Customers receiving a correct citation to a stale article | Retrieve only from the reviewed corpus; log `doc_id` on every answer so a bad article can be traced to every reply that used it |
| Customer text treated as an instruction | Medium | Severe | CloudServe's reputation | Ticket content fenced and passed as data, never as instruction; separate input validation that blocks |
| A ticket that should never be automated is automated | Medium | **Severe** | Customer in a security or compliance situation | Hard policy rules on intent, applied before any confidence is consulted, plus a veto when a never-automate intent is a close runner-up |
| Model provider unavailable | High | Low if handled | Nobody, if it degrades properly | Local grounded generation as a supported mode, not an emergency path |

## Section six: the problem statement

| Element | Statement | Supported by |
|---|---|---|
| What the client asked for | A chatbot to answer the easy tickets so agents can do the hard ones | Marcus, interview one |
| What the evidence suggests they actually need | A retrieval and routing layer that delivers answers which already exist in their documentation, and hands everything else to a person with the analysis attached | §2: 71.4% answerable; §1: Ines on why search fails; §1: Daniel on context-free escalations |
| The gap between those two | A chatbot generates answers. CloudServe do not lack answers — they lack delivery. A system that generates would invent answers to questions already answered, and would be confidently wrong on the 28.6% that have no documented answer | §2: 71.4% answerable against 43.8% resolved |
| Who is affected and how | Customers wait 8–12 hours for answers half of them could have found. Agents spend most of each ticket searching rather than answering. Tier 2 spends half its time on avoidable work. Standard and enterprise tiers fare worst | §1, §2, §3 |
| What will change if this is solved | FCR from 43.8% toward 60%+. Escalations carrying analysis rather than arriving bare. Agent time returned to the 20% of volume consuming 32% of effort | §3 |
| What is explicitly not in scope | Security incidents, compliance requests, feature requests and unclear tickets are never automated. No commitments about money, quotas or timelines. No learning from agents' private snippet files. No multi-turn conversation | §1 Daniel; §2 `must_not_auto_respond` at 100% for all four |

### The one paragraph version

> CloudServe's support team is not short of answers — it is short of a way to
> find them. Seven in ten of the tickets arriving each week are already answered
> somewhere in the twenty-nine support articles the company maintains, but fewer
> than half get resolved at first contact, because the only way to reach those
> articles is to already know which one you want. So agents rebuild answers from
> memory and from private notes, customers wait eight to twelve hours for
> something that was written down two years ago, and half of everything passed
> to a senior engineer turns out to be work that need never have been passed to
> anyone. The fix is not a machine that invents answers; it is a way of putting
> the right existing answer in front of the customer within seconds, being
> honest when there isn't one, and handing the genuinely difficult cases to a
> person with the thinking already done.

### Testing it against the five

- **Marcus** would recognise it and would be surprised by the seven-in-ten figure, since he said he would be guessing at the breakdown.
- **Sofia** would recognise it immediately — it is her account, quantified.
- **Daniel** would recognise the half-of-escalations claim as his own.
- **Ines** would recognise it as the thing she has been unable to get taken seriously.
- **Ravi** would recognise the waiting, though the framing is about the queue rather than his experience of it.

The statement is not one any of the five could have produced alone: Sofia knows
the volume is routine but not why the articles go unused, Ines knows why but
not the scale, Daniel sees the consequence but only at his end, and Marcus sees
the totals but nothing inside them.

### Open questions carried forward

1. Which CSAT figure is current, 3.2 or the measured 2.97?
2. Who is accountable for an automated answer that turns out to be wrong? Assumption: the Head of Support, pending confirmation.
3. What does a wrong automated answer actually cost? Marcus quantified escalations at 4× but not this. The threshold is sensitive to it, so it is carried as a parameter with published sensitivity.
4. Are the agents' private snippet files recoverable for review? If so, the answerable share may be higher than 71.4%.
